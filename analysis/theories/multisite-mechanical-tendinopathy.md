# Theory: Multisite Mechanical Tendinopathy And Load-Capacity Mismatch

Last updated: 2026-08-08

## Status

This is the leading explanation for the focal sit-bone/hamstring and medial-elbow symptoms. It may coexist with isotretinoin-associated musculoskeletal sensitivity, mechanical spinal referral and protective muscle guarding. It is not proven until a targeted examination reproduces and localizes the pain.

The working model is **bilateral compression-sensitive proximal hamstring tendinopathy or enthesopathy**, a separate rowing-related common-flexor/medial-elbow tendinopathy, and secondary lumbopelvic/upper-body guarding after tissue load exceeded current capacity.

## Why This Fits

- Pain is centered at the proximal hamstring/sit-bone region rather than a swollen joint.
- Proximal hamstring tendons are compressed against the ischial tuberosity by sitting, deep hip flexion, squats/lunges and end-range hamstring stretching. Those overlap the recorded triggers.
- Yoga/hamstring stretching aggravated symptoms, and a few light barbell squats on 2026-06-09 caused a disproportionate “pulled” feeling, pain and limping. Deep squat position can produce high tendon compression even when external weight is light.
- Pain was recorded as low at rest and higher with movement, with full range possible if forced. That is compatible with local tendon load intolerance.
- Medial-elbow symptoms began after rowing around August/September 2025, before the latest isotretinoin course. This gives one site a clear activity-linked mechanism.
- Deep massage reliably worsens symptoms, while heat helps. Direct pressure and forceful stretching can irritate a compression-sensitive tendon; heat can reduce guarding without proving inflammation.
- Tendon, bursal, nerve and mechanical spinal conditions normally leave CBC, ESR, CRP, CK, thyroid tests and autoimmune antibodies unchanged.

## Why This Is Not Yet Certain

- There is no documented sports-medicine examination of the ischial origins or medial elbow.
- Bilateral symptoms and broader neck/back/rib stiffness invite a systemic or medication-related contributor rather than one isolated injury.
- “Worse after inactivity” and morning stiffness can occur with both tendon stiffness and inflammatory disease.
- No dedicated pelvis/proximal-hamstring ultrasound or MRI is available.
- The 2026-06-09 squat episode could represent a partial strain/tear superimposed on pre-existing tendinopathy, particularly if there was a pop, bruising or lasting strength loss; those details are not documented.

## Targeted Examination

A sports-medicine physician, PM&R clinician or experienced musculoskeletal physical therapist should check:

- One-finger pain localization and palpation of both proximal hamstring origins/ischial tuberosities.
- Pain with resisted knee flexion at different knee angles, resisted hip extension, Puranen-Orava, bent-knee stretch and modified bent-knee stretch tests.
- Strength, single-leg bridge tolerance, gait and side-to-side power; bruising or palpable defect if tear is suspected.
- Ischial bursa pressure sensitivity and swelling.
- Hip range, FADIR/FABER/log roll; gluteal and adductor tendon loading.
- Lumbar repeated-movement response, strength, reflexes, sensation, straight-leg raise and slump.
- Deep-gluteal tests when pain is burning/electric, radiates down the posterior thigh or is accompanied by tingling/numbness.
- Medial elbow common-flexor origin, resisted wrist flexion/pronation, ulnar nerve symptoms, valgus stability, rowing grip and volume.

No single provocation test is definitive. A coherent cluster that reproduces the patient's familiar pain is more useful than a scan finding alone.

## Closest Mechanical Alternatives

- **Partial proximal hamstring strain/tear:** moves up with a pop, bruising, palpable defect, major strength loss or persistent limp.
- **Ischial bursitis:** moves up when direct sitting pressure and focal bursal tenderness dominate more than active hamstring loading.
- **Deep-gluteal/ischial-tunnel syndrome:** moves up with sciatic-type radiation, burning, tingling, numbness or neural-tension signs.
- **Lumbar somatic referral:** moves up when repeated lumbar movements reproduce, centralize or peripheralize the buttock/posterior-thigh pain.
- **Gluteal tendinopathy:** usually lateral hip pain and side-lying intolerance rather than sit-bone pain.
- **Hip joint disorder:** usually groin pain, catching/clicking or restricted/painful hip motion.
- **Mechanical SI pain:** usually localizes around the posterior superior iliac spine; a positive provocation-test cluster is more useful than tenderness alone.

## Imaging Path

- Imaging is not mandatory if the examination is convincing and symptoms improve with appropriate rehabilitation.
- If diagnosis remains uncertain, the acute squat event left weakness/limping, or an appropriate 8–12-week program fails, discuss expert musculoskeletal ultrasound or noncontrast pelvic MRI specifically covering both proximal hamstring origins, ischial bursae, deep-gluteal spaces and sciatic nerves.
- MRI findings require clinical correlation. Intratendinous signal can occur without symptoms; tendon enlargement, peritendinous edema and abnormalities matching the painful examination are more persuasive.
- Lumbar MRI is higher yield for persistent radiating pain, objective neurologic deficits or procedural planning than for diffuse stiffness alone.
- If the main unresolved question is inflammatory sacroiliitis, order a dedicated SI-joint inflammatory protocol rather than assuming a general pelvis scan will answer it.

## Treatment Path To Discuss

1. **Reduce compression without complete rest.** Temporarily limit prolonged hard-surface sitting, deep squats/lunges, long-stride hills, aggressive forward folds and direct deep massage over the painful origin. A pressure-dispersing cushion and frequent position changes may help.
2. **Start tolerable loading.** Progress from comfortable isometrics/short-range bridges or curls to slow resistance, then gluteal/lumbopelvic and whole-chain strength, later reintroducing deeper hip flexion and faster tasks.
3. **Dose by delayed response.** Symptoms should be near baseline by the following day. A material 12–24-hour flare means reducing load, depth, repetitions or frequency.
4. **Rebuild general capacity.** Include trunk, gluteal, hip abductor/adductor, calf and aerobic conditioning. Address the elbow separately with rowing-load/technique changes and progressive flexor-pronator loading.
5. **Escalate selectively.** A 2025 randomized trial found individualized physiotherapy and shockwave both improved proximal-hamstring outcomes, with no clear superiority. Evidence for PRP, autologous blood and corticosteroid injection is limited; injections and surgery should follow a clear diagnosis, not substitute for localization and rehabilitation.

## What Would Strengthen This Theory

- Familiar pain reproduced at the ischial origin by palpation, tendon loading or hip-flexion compression.
- A predictable same-day/next-day relationship to sitting, squat depth, hinging, lunges or stretching.
- Imaging abnormality matching the side/site and examination when imaging is warranted.
- Progressive improvement with load modification and graded strengthening.
- Separate reproducible common-flexor pain at the medial elbow after rowing load.

## What Would Weaken This Theory

- Repeated expert examination cannot reproduce symptoms from hamstring, hip, lumbar, SI, neural or elbow structures.
- Dominant second-half-of-night pain, alternating buttock pain, objective swelling/dactylitis, uveitis/IBD, reduced chest expansion or inflammatory imaging.
- Generalized allodynia, unrefreshing sleep, marked fatigue and cognitive symptoms dominate over local loading patterns.
- Fever, weight loss, progressive neurologic deficit or another red-flag cause emerges.
